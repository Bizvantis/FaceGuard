\begin{abstract}
Windows face sign-in requires an infrared camera because a photograph can deceive an ordinary RGB webcam, so most laptops offer no face-based protection. We present \sys{}, a CPU-only system that adds face recognition, a liveness check and continuous presence authentication to any webcam while leaving password or PIN sign-in untouched. We show that a steeply tilted print can push the five-landmark yaw estimate past a head-turn threshold, and we propose a minimal homography-parallax test that is exactly zero for planar presentations under an ideal pinhole camera with exact landmarks. In Monte-Carlo simulations on \parFacesEval{} held-out 3-D face meshes, a protocol combining this test with ordered, timed challenges rejected every flat and bent print and admitted \attUniversalFull\% of replays containing every action, against \lbUniMin\%--\lbUniMax\% for head-turn, blink and random-challenge baselines, while rejecting \livFrr\% of live users; real-time prompt-following replays still pass. A physical evaluation with a laptop webcam over \rwAllSessions{} sessions rejected all \rwAllAttN{} phone-screen attacks, but showed that the learned landmark detector reports parallax for tilted photographs that grows with their apparent yaw, so these attacks were stopped by the other checks rather than by the parallax gate. The recognition pipeline reaches \lfwAccFull\% verification accuracy on Labeled Faces in the Wild and an equal error rate of \mpEer\% on laptop-webcam images recorded over weeks, with a higher false-match rate for women than for men across three networks. In simulated webcam streams a presence guard caused no false lock in \gHours{}~h.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{L}{eaving} a workstation unlocked, even briefly, hands the whole session to whoever sits down next, including open e-mail, stored passwords and single-sign-on tokens. Idle timeouts and manual locking reduce this exposure, yet both depend on the user, and a timeout long enough to be tolerable is too long to stop an opportunistic insider. Continuous authentication re-checks the user's presence after login, for instance with soft biometrics seen by a webcam or with wrist-worn and wireless signals that reveal when the user walks away (Section~\ref{sec:related}); a recent review of biometric continuous authentication is given in~\cite{ayeswarya2024review}.

On a laptop the face is the obvious signal for this purpose: a camera already points at the user, and deep face recognition has become highly accurate~\cite{deng2019arcface}. Windows Hello, however, allows face sign-in only with a near-infrared (IR) camera, and it requires impostors to be accepted in fewer than 0.001\% of attempts while more than 95\% of genuine attempts succeed~\cite{ms2024hello}. Plain RGB webcams do not qualify because a printed photograph or a phone screen can stand in for the user~\cite{zhang2012casiafasd}, so the RGB camera found in most laptops cannot be used for face sign-in at all. Replacing the operating-system (OS) password with an RGB face match would make the machine easier, not harder, to break into.

\sys{} therefore keeps the password or PIN as the only way to unlock the OS and uses the webcam for protections that stay safe even if the face matcher is fooled: the PC locks when its owner leaves, a stranger who sits down is photographed and locked out, a person reading over the owner's shoulder triggers a warning, the locked screen stays watched, and user-level applications or scripts can request a liveness-checked face unlock.

A head-turn challenge needs an estimate of head yaw. When only the five landmarks returned by a face detector are available, the simplest estimate is the sideways offset of the nose from the midpoint of the eyes. We show analytically and in rendering experiments that a photograph tilted strongly towards the camera undergoes a keystone distortion that slants the eye line; when the estimator removes this apparent roll, part of the vertical nose offset becomes a sideways offset that resembles a real head turn. To close this gap we introduce a parallax test based on a four-point homography, which is exactly zero for a flat presentation however it is moved. Testing planarity is not new in itself: five coplanar points have two projective invariants, which De Marsico \emph{et al.} tracked over head motion to detect replayed photographs~\cite{demarsico2012projective}. Our formulation is a minimal one aimed at the failure above: the four nearly coplanar landmarks that a detector already returns predict the protruding fifth, the nose, and the signed residual, expressed in degrees like the yaw estimate, gates each prompted head turn against a frontal baseline. Throughout the paper we keep three kinds of evidence apart: an analytical property (planar invariance for an ideal pinhole camera and exact landmarks), Monte-Carlo simulations of the production code, and a physical evaluation with a laptop webcam, which shows that a learned landmark detector reports parallax for tilted planar presentations that grows with their apparent yaw.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A minimal landmark-based homography-parallax test for planar presentations: the roll-leakage analysis that motivates it, a signed residual with a proof of planar invariance under stated assumptions, and calibration and evaluation on disjoint sets of real 3-D face meshes.
\item Its integration into an ordered, timed challenge-response protocol for CPU-only webcams, evaluated by Monte-Carlo simulation against three conventional protocols and adaptive replay attackers, with an ablation of every defense, independent repetitions, multiplicity-corrected tests and a sensitivity analysis of thresholds and behavior.
\item A physical evaluation with \rwAllSessions{} recorded sessions and \rwAllAttN{} phone-screen attacks, and an analysis of the gap between simulated and real parallax that separates landmark-detector bias from the lens distortion of \pdCams{} calibrated laptop webcams.
\item \sys{}, a CPU-only implementation with open-set recognition, a presence guard, bounded template adaptation and encrypted templates. Its recognition accuracy on LFW and on laptop-webcam images, including demographic differentials with identity-level statistics, characterizes the deployed system and is not claimed as a contribution.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys prior work and Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the design of \sys{}. Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the data and protocols, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports and discusses the results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Face Detection and Recognition}
YuNet~\cite{wu2023yunet}, the face detector used by \sys{}, is an anchor-free network that predicts face boxes and five facial landmarks jointly and is designed for millisecond-level inference on small inputs; on 640-pixel frames of our laptop it takes \rtDet~ms (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}). Deep embeddings brought face verification on LFW to 99.63\% with the triplet objective of FaceNet~\cite{schroff2015facenet}, and training with an additive angular margin (ArcFace) became a common choice~\cite{deng2019arcface}. SFace~\cite{zhong2021sface} replaces hard margins by sigmoid constraints on intra- and inter-class distances so that low-quality training images are not over-fitted; we use its lightweight model from the OpenCV Model Zoo. Changes of lighting, pose and expression remain the main difficulty for such systems, and combining several classifiers by voting or bagging has been shown to raise recognition accuracy under them~\cite{gupta2024ensemble}; \sys{} instead pools the scores of a single network over the closest enrollment samples and over time (Section~\ref{sec:recog}).

\subsection{Presentation Attack Detection and Liveness}
Yu \emph{et al.}~\cite{yu2023fassurvey} review deep anti-spoofing methods. Passive detectors learn cues from single images, for example image-distortion statistics~\cite{wen2015ida} or depth maps supervised during training~\cite{liu2018auxiliary}; they need labeled attack data for training, and their accuracy on unseen sensors and attack types remains the main open problem (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

Motion- and geometry-based methods rely on the fact that a flat photograph moves differently from a 3-D head. Kollreider \emph{et al.} compare the optical flow of inner and outer face parts during small head rotations~\cite{kollreider2007motion}. De Marsico \emph{et al.} compute cross-ratios and the two projective invariants of five coplanar points from landmarks located by an active shape model, and accept a face as live when these invariants vary enough over a window of frames during head motion~\cite{demarsico2012projective}. Wang \emph{et al.} recover sparse 3-D facial structure from landmarks in key frames taken from two or more viewpoints and classify it with a support vector machine~\cite{wang2013structure}. Interactive schemes instead ask the user to blink~\cite{pan2007eyeblink} or to perform lip movements~\cite{zhou2024lipmotion}. Our test is closest to the five-point invariants of De Marsico \emph{et al.}: both check whether five image points are consistent with a plane. It differs in using only the five landmarks that the face detector already returns, in comparing each frame with a single frontal baseline instead of measuring variation over a window, in producing a signed angle that must agree with the prompted turn direction, and in acting as one gate of an ordered, timed challenge-response protocol rather than as a stand-alone classifier; Section~\ref{sec:parallax} compares the cues in detail. None of these methods, including ours, needs a personal 3-D model; Section~\ref{sec:liveres} compares our protocol experimentally with head-turn, blink and random-challenge baselines.

\subsection{Continuous Authentication and Template Management}
Niinuma \emph{et al.}~\cite{niinuma2010soft} keep a PC session authenticated by following the user's face and clothing color with a webcam, whereas ZEBRA~\cite{mare2014zebra} and FADEWICH~\cite{conti2017fadewich} end the session when a wristband or radio signals show that the user has left. \sys{} provides deauthentication and intruder capture with the built-in camera alone. Templates also need care after enrollment: updating them over time, as in template co-update~\cite{rattani2008coupdate}, follows appearance changes at the risk of drifting towards impostors, and stored templates must be protected; Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} explains how \sys{} bounds this drift and stores its templates.

\emph{Threat model.} We protect the unlocked OS session and, secondarily, the stored face templates, which together with the sensor are the attack points of a biometric system that concern us here~\cite{ratha2001enhancing}, against four adversaries: (A1) someone who sits down at an unattended, unlocked PC; (A2) someone at the locked PC, for instance guessing the PIN; (A3) a presentation attacker who wants face unlock to accept the owner, using a flat or bent print, a replayed recording of a genuine session, or a ``universal'' recording in which the owner performs every action, the kinds of presentation collected in face anti-spoofing datasets~\cite{zhang2012casiafasd}; and (A4) an onlooker behind the working owner. 3-D masks, injection of synthetic video into the camera driver and malware running with the user's rights are out of scope; such malware could disable any user-level program anyway. Because \sys{} never unlocks the OS, fooling its face matcher can at worst prevent a lock or open a user-level application. \subsection{Recognition Pipeline}\label{sec:recog}
\emph{Detection and alignment.} Each frame is scaled to a width of at most 640 pixels for YuNet~\cite{wu2023yunet} (model \texttt{face\_detection\_yunet\_2023mar}), which returns a box, a confidence and five points $\mathbf{p}_1,\dots,\mathbf{p}_5$: the right and left eye centers, the nose tip, and the right and left mouth corners, as seen from the subject. The face is warped to a $112\times112$ crop by the similarity transform $(s,\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t})$ that minimizes $\sum_i \lVert s\mathbf{R}\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{q}_i\rVert^2$ for canonical positions $\mathbf{q}_i$.

\emph{Embedding.} The SFace network~\cite{zhong2021sface} $\mathcal{F}$ (model \texttt{face\_recognition\_sface\_2021dec}, 38.7~MB) maps the crop $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$. With the normalization $\nu(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$ and $\mathrm{flip}(\cdot)$ the horizontal mirror image, enrollment and verification use flip test-time augmentation (TTA),
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}) = \nu\bigl(\nu(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}))+\nu(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{flip}(\mathbf{x})))\bigr),
\end{equation}
and faces are compared by their cosine similarity $s=\mathbf{e}_a^\top\mathbf{e}_b$. Live tracking skips the mirror image to save time.

\emph{Open-set matching.} \sys{} must recognize its users and also reject people who were never enrolled, the setting of open-set face recognition~\cite{gunther2017openset}. A person $p$ is enrolled with unit-norm samples $\{\mathbf{g}_{p,j}\}$, typically 22 images taken in five guided head poses. A query $\mathbf{q}$ receives the mean of its $k=3$ highest similarities to that person,
\begin{equation}
S_p(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_k(p)}\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{g}_{p,j},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_k(p)$ indexes these samples. Averaging the closest samples favors enrollment images whose pose resembles the query and is less noisy than a single nearest neighbor. The scores of a tracked face are smoothed by an exponential moving average, $\tilde{S}_p(t)=\alpha S_p(t)+(1-\alpha)\tilde{S}_p(t-1)$ with $\alpha=0.5$, and with $p^\star=\arg\max_p\tilde{S}_p$ the face is labeled
\begin{equation}
\text{label}=\begin{cases}
\text{match}, & \tilde{S}_{p^\star}\ge\tau,\\
\text{unknown}, & \tilde{S}_{p^\star}<\tau_u,\\
\text{uncertain}, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_u=0.30$ and $\tau$ equals 0.38, 0.45 or 0.52 for the relaxed, balanced (default) and strict security levels. Alarms can be raised only by \emph{unknown} faces and unlocking needs a \emph{match}, while ambiguous frames fall in the \emph{uncertain} band.

\emph{Tracking and quality gate.} Detections are linked to tracks greedily by intersection over union (IoU $\ge0.25$). If no box overlaps enough, a detection whose center lies within 0.6 face widths of a track and whose size differs by less than a factor of 1.5 continues that track, which handles fast motion at low frame rates; a track ends after five missed frames or 1~s. Each track is re-embedded every 0.35~s (0.2~s in the background). The quality gate, a cheap rule-based alternative to learned face-quality estimation, does not stop a face from being tracked and matched; it decides which faces may be enrolled, may update a template and may trigger an intruder lock. For enrollment it requires a face at least 80 pixels wide, a Laplacian variance of at least 35 (gray levels squared, on the $112\times112$ crop), a mean brightness between 45 and 200 gray levels with at most 12\% over-exposed pixels, a detector score of at least 0.8, $|\text{yaw}|\le40^\circ$, $|\text{pitch}|\le35^\circ$ and the whole face inside the frame. Passive decisions use 60 pixels, a Laplacian variance of 25 and $|\text{yaw}|,|\text{pitch}|\le30^\circ$. These limits are deliberately conservative relative to the degradation results of Section~\ref{sec:robust}, where recognition degrades noticeably below about 24 pixels and under over-exposure.

\subsection{Head-Pose Estimation and Roll Leakage}\label{sec:yaw}
Let $\mathbf{u}$ be the unit vector pointing from the right to the left eye, $d=\lVert\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_1\rVert$ the distance between the eyes and $\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{p}_1+\mathbf{p}_2)/2$ their midpoint. The estimator measures the roll $\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(u_y,u_x)$, rotates the nose offset back with the 2-D rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{-\phi}$, $\mathbf{n}'=\mathbf{R}_{-\phi}(\mathbf{p}_3-\mathbf{m})$, and converts it into a yaw ratio and angle
\begin{equation}
\gamma = n'_x/d,\qquad \hat{\theta}=\arctan(\gamma/K),
\end{equation}
where $K=\parKratio$ is fitted on a calibration set of real 3-D faces (Section~\ref{sec:pose}). On \poseN{} LFW photographs, $\hat\theta$ agrees with the 3-D head pose of the MediaPipe face mesh~\cite{kartynnik2019facemesh} with a Pearson correlation of \poseR{} and a mean absolute difference of \poseMae$^\circ$.

Turning a flat photograph by $\beta$ about a vertical axis does more than compress it horizontally. A pinhole camera~\cite{hartley2004mvg} with focal length $f$ at distance $D$ images the card point $(x,y)$ at $(f x\cos\beta,\, f y)/(D+x\sin\beta)$, which enlarges the nearer side and slants the eye line by an angle $\phi_\beta\neq0$. Undoing this slant also rotates the nose offset, whose vertical part $h$ is much larger than its horizontal part, and moves roughly $h\sin\phi_\beta$ into $n'_x$. As Fig.~\ref{fig:parallax}(a) shows, the average apparent yaw of a tilted print stays small, but individual faces reach \parFlatRatioMax$^\circ$ of apparent yaw change, enough to pass a head-turn prompt.

\subsection{Homography-Parallax Cue}\label{sec:parallax}
The eye centers and mouth corners lie close to one plane, whereas the nose tip sits on average \parNose~mm in front of the eye plane. Denote by $\mathbf{b}_i$ the landmarks recorded while the user looks straight at the camera, by $\mathbf{c}_i$ the current ones, and by $\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{h}}_i=[\mathbf{b}_i^\top,1]^\top$ their homogeneous coordinates. The homography $\mathbf{H}$ with $\mathbf{c}_i\simeq\mathbf{H}\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{h}}_i$ for $i\in\{1,2,4,5\}$, where $\simeq$ denotes equality up to scale, is obtained by the direct linear transform~\cite{hartley2004mvg}, and four correspondences determine it exactly. Mapping the baseline nose gives the prediction $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_3=\eta(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{h}}_3)$, where $\eta$ divides by the third coordinate, and we define the parallax ratio and the parallax yaw
\begin{equation}
\rho=\frac{(\mathbf{c}_3-\hat{\mathbf{c}}_3)^\top\mathbf{u}}{d},\qquad \psi=\arctan(\rho/K_p),
\end{equation}
with $K_p=\parKpar$ fitted on the calibration set.

\begin{proposition}
If the five landmarks lie on a plane, i.e.\ for any flat print or screen, and are observed without noise by a pinhole camera, then $\rho=0$ for every rigid motion of the plane.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A scene plane is mapped to the image by a homography, so the baseline and current views of the plane differ by some homography $\mathbf{H}_0$. Since four points in general position determine a homography uniquely, $\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}_0$. The nose lies on the same plane, hence $\mathbf{c}_3\simeq\mathbf{H}_0\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{h}}_3$ and $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_3=\mathbf{c}_3$.
\end{proof}

When a real head turns, the protruding nose leaves its predicted position and $\psi$ follows the rotation (Fig.~\ref{fig:parallax}(b)). The proposition rests on three assumptions: an ideal pinhole camera, exact landmarks, and five points that lie on one plane. Each is violated to some degree in practice. Lens distortion and landmark noise make $\rho$ nonzero for flat prints, which the acceptance threshold has to absorb; a print bent into a cylinder is no longer planar and produces some parallax, although far less than a head; and a learned landmark detector need not return the projections of the points of a planar face, which Section~\ref{sec:pose} identifies as the dominant effect on real hardware. Because a zero residual means that the five points keep their projective invariants between the baseline and the current view, the test is a minimal, signed form of the five-point planarity test of De Marsico \emph{et al.}; it reuses the detector's landmarks and costs one $8\times8$ linear solve per frame. Table~\ref{tab:geo} contrasts it with the motion- and geometry-based cues of Section~\ref{sec:related}: none of them needs a personal 3-D model, and ours uses the fewest points and no trained classifier.

\subsection{Challenge-Response Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
A face-unlock session (Algorithm~\ref{alg:live}) passes through the phases \emph{align}, \emph{challenge}$_i$, \emph{recenter} and \emph{final}. While aligning, the user looks at the camera for four frames, and the session stores the baseline yaw $\hat\theta_0$, the baseline blink and smile scores $b_0$ and $s_0$, the baseline landmarks $\mathbf{b}_i$ and a reference embedding. It then asks for 3 actions sampled without replacement from $\{$turn left, turn right, blink, smile$\}$ so that each sequence has at least one turn and one expression; without a face mesh only turns are asked for, never the same one twice in a row. The blink and smile are read from the blendshape scores of the face mesh~\cite{kartynnik2019facemesh}. A turn is accepted after two consecutive frames with $\pm(\hat\theta-\hat\theta_0)\ge\tau_\theta$ and $\pm\psi\ge0.8\,\tau_\theta$, where $\tau_\theta=18^\circ$. This turn threshold, the factor 0.8 and the other protocol thresholds are design constants that were set while the application was developed and kept unchanged for the experiments reported here, except for the blink thresholds discussed below; the only quantities fitted to data are $K$ and $K_p$, on calibration meshes disjoint from all evaluation data (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and Section~\ref{sec:liveres} reports how the results change over a range of each threshold. A blink must lift the blink score above $\max(0.30,b_0+0.20)$ and let it fall below $\max(0.20,b_0+0.10)$ again, and a smile must hold the smile score above $\max(0.5,s_0+0.3)$ for two frames. The thresholds are relative to the user's own baseline so that people with a high resting score are not rejected. The blink thresholds were lowered from 0.45 and 0.25 after the first round of the real-world evaluation showed that a natural blink sampled at webcam frame rates often peaks at only 0.3--0.4 (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}); their effect on live users is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:liveres}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Face-unlock liveness session}\label{alg:live}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frames with landmarks, yaw $\hat\theta$, blink and smile scores, embeddings
\Ensure \textsc{pass} with an identity, or \textsc{fail} with a reason
\State record $\hat\theta_0$, $b_0$, $s_0$, $\mathbf{b}_i$ and a reference embedding over 4 frontal frames
\State draw 3 distinct actions from \{left, right, blink, smile\}; strikes $\gets 0$
\For{each action $a$}
  \State show the prompt; $t_p\gets$ now
  \Repeat
    \IfThen{2nd face, face lost $>1$~s, or identity $<0.40$ within $20^\circ$}{\Return \textsc{fail}}
    \IfThen{off-prompt action starts from neutral}{strikes $\gets$ strikes $+1$}
    \IfThen{strikes $>1$}{\Return \textsc{fail}}
    \State count $a$ only if its onset ($\kappa>0.3$) is in $[t_p{+}0.15,\,t_p{+}3.5]$~s
    \IfThen{no valid onset by $t_p{+}3.5$~s, or $a$ lasts $>7$~s}{\Return \textsc{fail}}
  \Until{$a$ is accepted}
  \State wait until the face is centered again
\EndFor
\State \Return the matcher's decision on the mean frontal embedding
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\emph{Order enforcement.} A random sequence of actions, like the lip-motion patterns of~\cite{zhou2024lipmotion}, is meant to be hard to reproduce with a recording made in advance, which holds only if actions outside the requested order are penalized. Performing an action that belongs to another prompt, such as turning the wrong way, turning while a blink or smile is requested, or smiling during a turn or blink prompt, earns a strike. As the reflected view may lead one to confuse left and right, the first strike is excused, but the second strike ends the session. A blink does not count as a strike at all, and an additional strike is counted only after the face has returned to a neutral position.

\emph{Response timing.} Each action has to begin as a reaction to its prompt. Let the progress $\kappa(t)\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of the acceptance threshold that has been reached; for a turn it is the smaller of the yaw-ratio and parallax fractions. A movement counts only if $\kappa$ first exceeds 0.3 between $t_{\min}=0.15$~s and $t_{\max}=3.5$~s after the prompt, and the session fails if no such onset occurs by $t_{\max}$. Reaction times to visual stimuli measured with a high-speed camera are of the order of 0.3~s~\cite{tatsuno2016reaction}, and they vary between and within people; the window therefore starts below the fastest plausible reaction and leaves ample time for slow users. A looping video, in contrast, cannot anticipate a prompt, so movements already under way when the prompt appears, or starting too late, are ignored.

\emph{Identity continuity.} Every new embedding taken within $20^\circ$ of the baseline pose must keep a cosine similarity of at least 0.40 with the reference, well below typical genuine scores but above most impostor scores (Section~\ref{sec:results}-A). Embeddings of strongly turned faces legitimately differ from a frontal one (the first real-world round rejected a genuine user at $31^\circ$--$49^\circ$), so during a turn the tracker carries the identity, and a lost track forces a new check. A second face, losing the face for more than 1~s, spending more than 7~s on one action or exceeding 45~s in total fails the session. The final decision is taken by the matcher on the mean of the frontal embeddings gathered during the session.

\subsection{Presence Guard}\label{sec:guard}
The guard (Fig.~\ref{fig:guard}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}) analyzes four frames per second in the background. Like the soft-biometric session monitoring of~\cite{niinuma2010soft}, it re-checks the user continuously, but it identifies every face in view, which enables the intruder and onlooker rules below. A face counts as \emph{authorized} when it matches the owner or a trusted person, and as a \emph{stranger} when, after at least two embeddings, it is unknown or matches an enrolled person who is not trusted, so that a single bad frame cannot raise an alarm. With $t_a$ the last time an authorized face was seen and $I(t)$ the keyboard and mouse idle time, the guard applies the following rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Walk-away lock} once $\min(t-t_a,\,I(t))\ge T_a$ ($T_a=30$~s by default), announced 8~s beforehand; while the user types, the PC stays unlocked even if the face is out of view.
\item \emph{Intruder}: a stranger face that passes the passive quality gate and is present for $T_i=2.5$~s, with no authorized face during the last 3~s, is photographed and reported, and the PC is locked.
\item \emph{Shoulder-surfer}: an authorized user together with a stranger at least 40 pixels wide for 3~s produces a notification; because only a notification follows, the quality gate is not required.
\item \emph{Lock-screen watch}: for $W$ minutes after locking (30 by default), confirmed strangers are photographed; otherwise the camera is released while the PC is locked.
\item \emph{Safety valves}: for 15~s after each unlock, intruder detections only suggest re-enrollment; at most three intruder locks are issued within 10 minutes; a camera failure never locks the PC; alerts are limited to one per minute.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Template Adaptation, Storage and Implementation}\label{sec:adaptive}
Faces change over time, and template co-update~\cite{rattani2008coupdate} showed how templates can follow them. \sys{} adapts its templates in a bounded way: it appends a live embedding only when its smoothed score reaches $\tau+0.12$, the face passes the quality gate and is near-frontal, its highest similarity to the stored samples is below 0.92 so that it adds information, and no update happened during the last 10 minutes. Adaptive samples are kept in a first-in-first-out buffer of at most $\min(30,\max(5,n_e))$ entries, $n_e$ being the number of enrolled samples, so they never outnumber an enrollment of five or more samples (Section~\ref{sec:webcam} evaluates the rule over weeks). Stored templates are an attack target in their own right~\cite{ratha2001enhancing}. Whereas biometric data that leave the device can be protected by hiding them in a cover image and encrypting them before they are sent to a cloud service~\cite{prabhu2022stego}, \sys{} keeps its templates on the device: templates, names and interface thumbnails are serialized, compressed and encrypted with the Windows Data Protection API, whose user-scope key is derived from the user's logon credentials, so the file can be decrypted only by the same user on the same machine.\footnote{CryptProtectData function (dpapi.h), Microsoft Learn: \url{https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dpapi/nf-dpapi-cryptprotectdata}} Files are written atomically with a backup copy, and video is never stored.

The software is written in Python~3.12 with OpenCV~5.0, NumPy~2.5, MediaPipe~1.0.1, scikit-learn~1.9.1 and PySide6~6.11. OpenCV runs the YuNet (0.23~MB) and SFace models in the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format, the MediaPipe Face Landmarker (\texttt{face\_landmarker.task}, 3.8~MB) supplies 478 points and 52 blendshape scores, and PySide6 draws the interface. Ctypes is used to access the Windows functions for locking, session state, idle time and DPAPI, so no admin rights are needed. Every model is verified against a pinned SHA-256 hash after download. The application offers a live view with landmark, mesh and head-pose overlays, guided enrollment, face unlock, people management, the guard, an intruder gallery and a ``Model Lab'' that shows leave-one-out accuracy, score histograms and an embedding map of the user's own gallery (Fig.~\ref{fig:screens}). All functions are also available from the command line; \texttt{faceguard verify}, for example, exits with code 0 only for a live, recognized face and can thus protect user scripts. The camera is opened at $640\times480$ by default: the built-in webcam of our laptop delivered only 10 frames per second at $1280\times720$ but 20 at $640\times480$, and blink detection needs the frame rate more than the resolution. The code base has about ten thousand lines of Python and 110 automated tests.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\emph{Platform.} Every experiment ran on a Windows~11 laptop with an eight-core AMD Ryzen~7 7730U processor and 16~GB of memory, using the CPU only, with the software versions listed in Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} and onnxruntime~1.30 for the baselines.

\emph{Data.} The \lfwImages{} photographs of Labeled Faces in the Wild (LFW)~\cite{huang2007lfw} show \lfwPeople{} individuals. YuNet found a face in every image; in the \lfwMultiFace{} images with several faces, the one nearest to the image center was used. In the median, faces are \lfwFaceWidth{} pixels wide and turned by \lfwYawMedian$^\circ$. For the geometry and liveness experiments we sampled 1,500 LFW images and kept the first \parFaces{} for which the MediaPipe mesh reported $|\text{yaw}|\le10^\circ$ and $|\text{pitch}|\le15^\circ$; their meshes were split at random into a calibration set of \parFacesCal{} faces, used only to fit $K$ and $K_p$, and a disjoint evaluation set of \parFacesEval{} faces, used for every geometry and liveness result. The head-pose agreement was measured on the \poseN{} of 3,000 randomly sampled LFW images for which MediaPipe returned a mesh. To evaluate recognition under the target condition, we use MPIIFaceGaze~\cite{zhang2017mpiifacegaze}, which contains frames recorded by the built-in webcams of \mpPeople{} participants' own laptops during everyday use over \mpDaysMin--\mpDaysMax{} days; we sampled \mpPer{} frames per participant evenly over their recording days (\mpImages{} frames, median face width \mpFacePx{} pixels). For the demographic analysis we label LFW identities with the attribute classifiers of Kumar \emph{et al.}~\cite{kumar2011attributes} (Section~\ref{sec:demo}).

\emph{Protocols.} Verification uses the ten View-2 splits of LFW, each holding 300 same-person and 300 different-person pairs; the threshold is fitted on nine splits and tested on the tenth. The ArcFace baselines (InsightFace models \texttt{w600k\_mbf} and \texttt{w600k\_r50}, trained on a WebFace-derived set~\cite{zhu2021webface260m}) receive the same detections and landmarks as SFace, aligned to their own canonical template, and the same flip TTA. Operating points are measured on all \opGenuine{} same-person pairs (at most 50 images per person) and on \opImpostor{} randomly drawn different-person pairs. Identification involves the \idPeople{} people with ten or more images (\idImages{} images in total) and $g$ enrollment images each, averaged over five repetitions. For the open-set test, half of these people are enrolled with five images, and the probes are their remaining images (\osKnown{} on average) together with every image of the others (\osUnknown{}). Robustness is measured on the \robPairs{} pairs of View-2 splits 1 and 2 with only the probe degraded; in addition, every degraded probe is compared with \robImpGallery{} clean images of other people, giving \robImpN{} impostor comparisons per condition. On MPIIFaceGaze, verification uses all same-person pairs recorded on different days and all different-person pairs, and the enrollment protocol of \sys{} enrolls each participant with 22 frames from their first recording days, probes with frames from the second half of their days and measures false accepts by comparing each probe with the galleries of the other 14 participants. Liveness is assessed by Monte-Carlo simulation (Section~\ref{sec:liveres}) and the guard with simulated webcam streams (Section~\ref{sec:guardres}). Runtimes are means of 200 runs after 20 warm-up runs on an otherwise idle machine; the CPU load of the live view was measured with a synthetic 30-frame-per-second image stream instead of a physical camera.

\emph{Real-world evaluation.} The production code was also run on the laptop's built-in webcam by a guided test program that records only decisions, scores, timings and, for the owner, the five landmark positions, but no images. The owner (one of the authors, enrolled with 22 images) performed face-unlock sessions with the full and the turn-only protocol, and presented their own frontal photograph on a phone screen, tilting and turning it as requested, against the full protocol and against the turn-only protocol with and without the parallax gate. Three volunteers who were not enrolled attempted face unlock and took part in the guard scenarios, in which the guard ran with the production thresholds but only logged its decisions. The evaluation ran in two rounds with the same test program. Round~1 (\rwPilotTrials{} sessions) used the earlier protocol at $1280\times720$, where the webcam delivered about 10 frames per second, and exposed the faults described in Section~\ref{sec:pilot}; round~2 used the corrected protocol at $640\times480$ (about \rwFps{} frames per second), and after every attack the owner confirmed that only the phone had been visible. Of the \rwAllSessions{} recorded sessions, one round-1 attack in which the owner's own face had been in view was discarded; all others are reported.

\emph{Reproducibility.} Table~\ref{tab:units} lists, for every experiment, the data, the experimental unit, the counts and the seeds or calibration data. Every randomized step uses a fixed seed: 2026 for the impostor pairs, the identification repetitions, the calibration/evaluation split, the MPIIFaceGaze sample, the demographic impostor pairs and the bootstraps, 0 for the mesh selection, 17 for the robustness impostor gallery, 70 and 7 for the two guard scenarios, one recorded seed per Monte-Carlo configuration, and the master seeds 1001--1005 for the repeated Monte-Carlo runs. No liveness threshold was tuned on the data used to evaluate it: $K$ and $K_p$ come from the calibration meshes, and the blink thresholds changed after round~1 of the real-world evaluation were evaluated only on round~2 and in new simulations.

\emph{Metrics.} Verification is summarized by accuracy, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), the equal error rate (EER) and the true-accept rate (TAR) at a fixed false-accept rate (FAR); the false-reject rate (FRR) is the complement of the TAR. Open-set identification is measured by the detection-and-identification rate (DIR) at a given false-alarm rate~\cite{gunther2017openset}. For liveness we use the terms of ISO/IEC 30107-3~\cite{iso30107-3}: APCER is the fraction of attacks accepted and BPCER the fraction of live users rejected.

\emph{Statistics.} Pairs that share people are not independent, so the 95\% intervals of pair-based error rates come from an identity-level bootstrap (\bsB{} replicates) that redraws people with replacement and weights every pair by the draw counts of its people. Monte-Carlo rates are repeated with five independent master seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds, with an interval from a bootstrap over the \msFaces{} evaluation faces, because sessions that share a face are correlated. Other proportions carry 95\% Wilson intervals, and when no event occurs in $n$ independent trials the 95\% upper bound is $3/n$ (``rule of three''). Differences between protocol variants are tested with two-sided Fisher exact tests and accuracy differences with paired $t$-tests over the ten identity-disjoint LFW folds. The eight liveness comparisons and the three recognition comparisons form two families whose $p$-values are Holm-adjusted; we report these adjusted values together with the effect size, a difference in percentage points with its 95\% interval. Of the liveness comparisons, two are primary: the parallax gate lowers the success of tilted flat prints in the turn-only protocol, and order enforcement with response timing lowers the success of universal replay.

\emph{Ethics.} Apart from the public LFW and MPIIFaceGaze images and a public-domain portrait, the only human data are those of the real-world evaluation, a minimal-risk test with four adult participants, one of them an author. All gave informed consent to take part and to the publication of the anonymous results; no image or video was stored, only decisions, similarity scores, timings and, for the author, the positions of five facial landmarks. The authors' institution has no institutional ethics committee, so this minimal-risk evaluation was conducted without formal ethics review. In deployment, photographs of strangers stay on the user's computer with a retention limit, and users should inform people who share their workspace.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Verification, Baselines and Operating Points}
Table~\ref{tab:lfw} compares SFace with two ArcFace models run through exactly the same pipeline, and Fig.~\ref{fig:roc} shows the ROC curves of the SFace variants. SFace reaches \lfwAccFull\% accuracy, an EER of \lfwEerFull\% and \lfwTarThreeFull\% TAR at $10^{-3}$ FAR. Both baselines perform better: the ArcFace~\cite{deng2019arcface} ResNet-50 (\baseRfiftyMb~MB) reaches \baseRfiftyAcc\% (difference to SFace \effSfaceRfifty{} points, Holm-adjusted paired $t$-test over the ten folds $p=\pSfaceRfifty$), and the smaller ArcFace MobileFaceNet (\baseMbfMb~MB) reaches \baseMbfAcc\% (\effSfaceMbf{} points, $p=\pSfaceMbf$), a difference that is not significant although its EER (\baseMbfEer\%) and its TAR at $10^{-3}$ FAR (\baseMbfTarThree\%) are also better. Recognition accuracy is therefore not a contribution of this work. \sys{} nevertheless uses SFace because it is distributed under the Apache~2.0 license and runs in OpenCV without further dependencies, whereas the InsightFace models are released for non-commercial research only\footnote{License terms as stated at \url{https://github.com/opencv/opencv_zoo} (SFace, Apache~2.0) and \url{https://github.com/deepinsight/insightface} (model zoo, non-commercial research purposes only), accessed Oct.~1, 2026.}; since the embedding network is a configuration choice, a deployment that may use them would gain accuracy after recalibrating the thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:system}. Mirroring at test time raises the accuracy of SFace by \effTta{} points, a difference that is not significant ($p=\pTta$), and lowers the EER from \lfwEerNoTTA\% to \lfwEerFull\%. Replacing landmark alignment with a plain resize of the detector box cuts the accuracy to \lfwAccNoAlign\%, so the five landmarks matter for recognition as much as for liveness. Because LFW is nearly saturated and its public figures may appear in the web-collected data used to train these networks~\cite{zhu2021webface260m}, the results are best read as an upper bound, which the robustness and system tests below complement.

\subsection{Identification and Open-Set Recognition}
Closed-set identification is already \idOneNn\% correct with a single enrollment image and levels off near 99.9\% with two or more. Classifiers trained on the embeddings bring no meaningful gain: with five enrollment images a linear support vector machine reaches \clsSvm\%, logistic regression \clsLogreg\% and $k$-nearest neighbors \clsKnn\%, against \clsTopthree\% for the top-3 rule, and unlike the similarity rules they must be retrained for every new person and cannot reject strangers by design. The open-set test, which follows the evaluation of~\cite{gunther2017openset} with unseen people among the probes, separates the rules more clearly (Fig.~\ref{fig:openset}, Table~\ref{tab:openset}). At the balanced threshold, the top-3 rule falsely accepts \osTopthreeFarBalanced\% of unseen people, about half the rate of the nearest-neighbor rule (\osNnFarBalanced\%), while identifying \osTopthreeDirBalanced\% of enrolled probes; at the strict level its false-alarm rate is \osTopthreeFarStrict\%. The centroid rule attains the highest DIR at low false-alarm rates but depends on a representative enrollment, whereas the top-3 mean copes better with atypical samples and is therefore the default. False alarms are more frequent here than in Table~\ref{tab:op} because each probe is compared with every enrolled person, which is why the guard requires unknown faces to persist and face unlock requires a live, smoothed match.

\subsection{Robustness to Degradations}\label{sec:robust}
Fig.~\ref{fig:robust} shows verification with degraded probes at the balanced threshold, where clean probes give \robClean\% TAR. Lighting is a known difficulty for face recognition~\cite{gupta2024ensemble}, but darkness hardly matters here (\robGainLow\% TAR at 15\% brightness), whereas over-exposure does (\robGainThree\% at three times the brightness), which motivates the asymmetric exposure limits of the quality gate. Recognition holds for faces down to about \robScaleQuarterPx{} pixels in width (\robScaleQuarter\% TAR), while detection breaks down below roughly \robScaleSmallPx{} pixels, where only \robScaleSmallDet\% of faces are found. Gaussian blur of 4 pixels, JPEG quality 4 and noise with a standard deviation of 32 gray levels reduce the TAR to \robBlurFour\%, \robJpegFour\% and \robNoiseThirtyTwo\%, respectively. A mask-like occluder over the lower face keeps \robMask\% TAR and sunglasses \robSunglasses\%, with AUCs of \robMaskAuc{} and \robSunglassesAuc{}, so occlusion harms a fixed threshold more than it harms the ranking of scores. Where masks are common, a model that recognizes masked faces from the upper half of the face, such as the VGG16 hybrid of Sikha and Bharath~\cite{sikha2022masked}, would be the better choice.

\subsection{Laptop-Webcam Images}\label{sec:webcam}
Table~\ref{tab:mpii} reports recognition on the MPIIFaceGaze frames~\cite{zhang2017mpiifacegaze}, which resemble the use of \sys{} far more closely than LFW does: one person at a desk, a fixed built-in camera and lighting that changes over days and weeks. Across \mpGen{} same-person pairs from different days and \mpImp{} different-person pairs, SFace reaches an AUC of \mpAuc{} and an EER of \mpEer\%, with \mpTarThree\% TAR at $10^{-3}$ FAR. At the balanced threshold \mpFaBal{} impostor pairs (\mpFarBal\%, identity-level 95\% interval \bsMpPairFarBalancedLo--\bsMpPairFarBalancedHi\%) were accepted and \mpFrrBal\% of genuine pairs (\bsMpPairFrrBalancedLo--\bsMpPairFrrBalancedHi\%) rejected, and at the strict threshold no impostor pair was accepted. Under the enrollment protocol of \sys{}, all \mpProbes{} probes from the later recording days were accepted at the balanced level and none of the leave-one-person-out impostor probes was (highest impostor score \mpEnrollImpMax). At the relaxed level, however, \mpEnrollFarRelaxed\% of impostor probes (\bsMpEnrFarRelaxedLo--\bsMpEnrFarRelaxedHi\%) exceeded the threshold, which argues against the relaxed level when several people are enrolled. The ArcFace models are again slightly more accurate. Because all of these pairs come from only \mpPeople{} people, the intervals are wide and the false-accept estimates less general than the LFW ones, but the results show that the pipeline transfers to everyday laptop-webcam images and to appearance changes over weeks.

\emph{Template adaptation.} Template updating can drift towards impostors~\cite{rattani2008coupdate}. To test the bounded update rule of Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} over time, each participant was enrolled as above, the other frames of the first half of their recording days were streamed in chronological order through the production update rule, and the frames of the second half served as probes. Because the frames carry no clock time, at most one update per recording day was allowed, fewer than the application permits. The rule added \adUpdates{} of \adStream{} streamed frames to the templates, which raised the mean genuine score of the \adProbes{} later probes from \adGenStatic{} to \adGenAdapted{} and left the strict-level TAR at \adStrictTarAdapted\%. At the balanced level no impostor probe was accepted before or after adaptation (highest leave-one-person-out impostor score \adImpMaxStatic{} and \adImpMaxAdapted{}), but at the relaxed level impostor acceptance rose from \adRelFarStatic\% to \adRelFarAdapted\%, a further reason to avoid that level. Without any rate limit (\adUpdatesStress{} updates) the outcome was the same at the balanced level. To emulate template poisoning, all \adPoisonOffered{} frames of the other participants were offered to each template through the same rule: none was added, because the highest impostor score, \adPoisonMax, stays well below the update gate of 0.57.

\subsection{Demographic Differentials}\label{sec:demo}
Table~\ref{tab:demo} reports error rates per demographic group. The groups come from the automatic attribute classifiers of Kumar \emph{et al.}~\cite{kumar2011attributes}, averaged over each person's images (\dmLabeled{} people labeled; people with ambiguous scores are left out of that attribute, and the group labeled Indian, with 105 people, is too small to analyze), and impostor pairs are drawn within each group, the harder and more relevant case~\cite{drozdowski2020bias}. Because pairs of the same people are correlated and the groups differ greatly in size, the 95\% intervals come from an identity-level bootstrap in which every group of an attribute is redrawn with the same number of people (\dbBalSex{} for sex and \dbBalEth{} for perceived ethnicity), so that groups are compared at equal sample sizes. To separate the network from the threshold, each of the three backbones of Table~\ref{tab:lfw} is also evaluated at its own threshold for an overall FMR of $10^{-4}$ on the general LFW impostor pairs (SFace \dbSfThr, ArcFace MobileFaceNet \dbMbfThr, ArcFace ResNet-50 \dbRfThr).

The higher false-match rate (FMR) of women is robust: at the matched operating point it is \dbSfFemaleRatio{} times that of men for SFace (95\% interval \dbSfFemaleRatioLo--\dbSfFemaleRatioHi), and \dbMbfFemaleRatio{} and \dbRfFemaleRatio{} times for the two ArcFace models, whose intervals also exclude parity; women also have a higher false-non-match rate (FNMR) with SFace. For perceived ethnicity the point estimates of the FMR are higher for the groups labeled Black and Asian than for the group labeled White with every backbone, but at equal group sizes the identity-level intervals are wide and overlap: for SFace the Black-to-White ratio of \dbSfBlackRatio{} has a lower bound of \dbSfBlackRatioLo, which includes parity, whereas for the ArcFace models the lower bounds are \dbMbfBlackRatioLo{} and \dbRfBlackRatioLo. This benchmark therefore establishes a sex differential and suggests, without establishing, an ethnicity differential. Because the same pattern appears with three networks trained on different data, it cannot be attributed to SFace in particular; whether it stems from the training data of face networks in general, from the composition and automatic labeling of LFW, or from the choice of threshold is a hypothesis that this benchmark cannot resolve. For \sys{} the differentials matter mainly through the guard, because a higher FMR within a group makes it more likely that a stranger is taken for an enrolled person of the same group. A benchmark with balanced, self-reported labels is needed to confirm the magnitudes, and group-aware thresholds or a network trained on balanced data are possible mitigations~\cite{drozdowski2020bias}.

\subsection{Pose and Parallax Geometry}\label{sec:pose}
To examine both cues with known ground-truth rotation, we reconstructed the 478-point MediaPipe meshes~\cite{kartynnik2019facemesh} described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, removed the head pose of each photograph by rotation-only Procrustes alignment to the mean shape, scaled each mesh to an eye distance of 63~mm and rendered it with a pinhole camera~\cite{hartley2004mvg} ($f=457$~pixels, i.e.\ a 640-pixel image with a $70^\circ$ field of view) at 50~cm, adding 1-pixel noise to the five landmarks. Every face was rendered as a real head turned by $\beta$, as a flat print and as prints bent into cylinders of radius 60, 40 and 30~mm, all rotated by the same $\beta\in[-60^\circ,60^\circ]$. Fitting the head rotations within $\pm30^\circ$ of the \parFacesCal{} calibration faces gave $K=\parKratio$ and $K_p=\parKpar$; the same fit on the \parFacesEval{} evaluation faces gives \parKratioEval{} and \parKparEval{}, so the constants do not depend on the faces they were fitted on. On the evaluation faces, a $25^\circ$ head turn reads $\parHeadRatioTwentyFive^\circ$ from the yaw ratio and $\parHeadParTwentyFive^\circ$ from the parallax (Fig.~\ref{fig:parallax}).

For flat prints the yaw ratio reached \parFlatRatioMax$^\circ$ of apparent yaw change, whereas the parallax never exceeded \parFlatParMax$^\circ$ (99th percentile \parFlatParQ$^\circ$), which is landmark noise below the $14.4^\circ$ parallax threshold. Bent prints produce some parallax, but far less than heads: at $25^\circ$ they read \parBentSixtyParTwentyFive$^\circ$, \parBentFortyParTwentyFive$^\circ$ and \parBentThirtyParTwentyFive$^\circ$ for radii of 60, 40 and 30~mm, compared with \parHeadParTwentyFive$^\circ$ for a head, and they cross the threshold only at extreme angles that also foreshorten the face. On real photographs, where no ground truth is available, the estimate agrees with the mesh-based head pose with a Pearson correlation of \poseR{} for yaw but only \posePitchR{} for pitch, so pitch is used only to guide enrollment. Under sensor noise of 4 gray levels the yaw of a still face fluctuates by \poseJitter$^\circ$ on average (95th percentile \poseJitterQ$^\circ$).

\emph{Simulated versus physical parallax.} The production engine recorded the apparent yaw change and the parallax of every frame of the real-world turn challenges (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}): \prPhoneFrames{} frames of \prPhoneSessions{} phone-screen attacks and \prOwnerFrames{} frames of \prOwnerSessions{} genuine sessions. Fig.~\ref{fig:parallaxreal} compares them with the rendered presentations. In simulation the parallax of a flat print is pure landmark noise: it does not grow with the apparent yaw, and with 1-pixel noise its 90th percentile stays below \prSimFlatPninety$^\circ$ in every yaw bin. The real phone screen behaves differently: its parallax grows almost in proportion to its apparent yaw, with medians of \prPhoneMedFive$^\circ$, \prPhoneMedTen$^\circ$ and \prPhoneMedFifteen$^\circ$ at apparent yaw changes of 5--10$^\circ$, 10--15$^\circ$ and 15--20$^\circ$, against \prOwnerMedFive$^\circ$, \prOwnerMedTen$^\circ$ and \prOwnerMedFifteen$^\circ$ for real head turns. Fitted through the origin over 5--20$^\circ$, the parallax rises by \prPhoneSlope{} degree per degree of apparent yaw for the phone (Pearson correlation \prPhoneR) and \prOwnerSlope{} for real heads, a ratio of \prSlopeRatio.

We examined three explanations. Lens distortion is not one: rendering the meshes through the calibrated intrinsics and distortion coefficients of the \pdCams{} laptop webcams of MPIIFaceGaze~\cite{zhang2017mpiifacegaze}, without landmark noise, adds at most \pdCentreMax$^\circ$ of parallax to a flat print at the image center and \pdOffMax$^\circ$ (median over cameras \pdOffMedian$^\circ$) when the face is 30\% of the half-width off center. Isotropic landmark noise is not one either: the noise level that best matches the upper percentiles of the real phone parallax is about \prEffNoise~px, and reproducing the real slope would need about 5~px, at which the 90th percentile at small yaw (\prPninetyFiveNoise$^\circ$) would far exceed the measured \prPhonePninetySmall$^\circ$. The explanation that remains consistent with the data is a systematic, yaw-dependent bias of the learned five-point detector: on a foreshortened photograph it appears to place the landmarks partly where they would be on a turned 3-D head, so the residual of a planar presentation tracks its apparent rotation. Proposition~1 still holds for the true projections, but the detector does not return them. With the ideal model, the parallax gate separates flat prints from heads by a wide margin (Section~\ref{sec:liveres}); with this detector on real hardware a tilted phone reaches about \prSlopeRatioPct\% of the parallax of a real head at the same apparent yaw, so in the physical attacks the yaw threshold, the expression prompts and the identity check carried the protection (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}). Landmarks that measure image motion rather than predicting face geometry, for example tracked feature points, are the natural remedy and remain to be evaluated.

\subsection{Liveness Evaluation}\label{sec:liveres}
Public attack datasets do not contain free head motion in response to prompts, so we tested the protocol by Monte-Carlo simulation of the production code. Landmarks were rendered at 20 frames per second from the evaluation meshes, with subjects 40--70~cm from the camera, a lateral offset of up to 4~cm and the head facing the camera, and blink and smile scores were simulated. Simulated users reacted after log-normal delays (median 0.7~s, longer than simple visual reaction times of about 0.3~s~\cite{tatsuno2016reaction} to allow for reading the prompt), turned by $30^\circ\pm8^\circ$ at random speed, turned further or blinked harder when the progress bar stalled, blinked spontaneously and mixed up left and right in 5\% of turn prompts. The print attacker tilted the photograph towards the requested side up to a random limit between $40^\circ$ and $75^\circ$. The replay attacker looped a recording of the owner completing one random sequence, and the universal-replay attacker looped a recording in which the owner cycles through all four actions. Each print configuration was attacked \attPrintN{} times and the two replay attacks were run \attReplayN{} and \attUniversalN{} times; live users were simulated in \livN{} sessions for the default protocol and in \livNAbl{} sessions per ablation.

\emph{Comparison with conventional protocols.} Table~\ref{tab:baselines} evaluates three conventional protocols in the same simulation: three head turns checked with the yaw ratio only, a single blink prompt in the spirit of~\cite{pan2007eyeblink}, and three random actions checked with the yaw ratio but without order enforcement or timing. The head-turn protocol accepts every live user but also \lbYawFlat\% of tilted flat prints and \lbYawBent\% of bent prints. Expression prompts stop prints, but all three conventional protocols are vulnerable to replay: a universal recording passes \lbUniMin\%--\lbUniMax\% of their sessions and a recording of one genuine session \lbRepMin\%--\lbRepMax\%. \sys{} rejects every print and lowers these rates to \lbFgUni\% (95\% interval \attUniversalFullLo--\attUniversalFullHi\%) and \lbFgReplay\% at a BPCER of \lbFgLive\%, slightly higher than that of the baselines (\lbYawLive--\lbBlinkLive\%). Repeating the principal configurations with five independent seeds gives the same picture: universal replay succeeds in \msUniMean{}~$\pm$~\msUniSd\% of sessions (mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over seeds; face-level bootstrap interval \msUniLo--\msUniHi\%) against \msUniNeitherMean{}~$\pm$~\msUniNeitherSd\% without order enforcement and timing, a looped genuine recording in \msRepMean{}~$\pm$~\msRepSd\%, and live users are rejected in \msLiveMean{}~$\pm$~\msLiveSd\% (\msLiveLo--\msLiveHi\%); none of the \msPrintZero{} flat- and bent-print sessions against the full protocol succeeded.

Against a universal replay, either defense alone is effective: the success rate falls from \attUniversalNoOrderNoTiming\% with neither to \attUniversalNoTiming\% with order enforcement only and \attUniversalNoOrderEnforcement\% with response timing only, and to \attUniversalFull\% with both (differences of the full protocol to these three variants: \effUniFullNeither, \effUniFullNoTiming{} and \effUniFullNoOrder{} points, Holm-adjusted Fisher exact tests $p\pUniFullNeither$, $p\pUniFullNoTiming$ and $p\pUniFullNoOrder$). \emph{Adaptive replay.} These replays are non-interactive. A stronger attacker owns several recordings of each action and edits or times them. We simulated an attacker with five clips per action, each played at a random speed between 0.8 and 1.25 times the original. Played blind as a pre-edited video with three distinct actions in random order, random gaps and a random start, the clips passed \arBlindFull\% of \arN{} full-protocol sessions (\arBlindTurns\% in turn-only mode), close to the chance of guessing one of the \seqDistinct{} sequences (min-entropy \seqMinEnt{} bits; \seqDistinctTurns{} sequences in turn-only mode) and hitting the response window. When an operator watches the prompts and starts the matching clip after a human reaction delay, \arHumanFull\% of the sessions pass (\arHumanTurns\% in turn-only mode), and \arAutoFull\% when software reads the prompt and reacts within 0.2--0.45~s. Such a prompt-following replay is, to the protocol, a live performance of the requested actions, so challenge-response liveness on RGB video protects only against non-interactive presentations; an attacker who can control the displayed video in real time, like one who injects video into the camera driver, is outside its reach and requires IR or depth sensing, a passive presentation-attack detector~\cite{yu2023fassurvey} or a hardware-backed camera path.

\subsection{Presence Guard}\label{sec:guardres}
The guard was run with the production engine and thresholds on simulated 4-frame-per-second webcam streams, made by pasting LFW photographs into $640\times480$ frames with random shifts, gain changes and sensor noise. Continuous-authentication studies mostly report FAR, FRR and EER, whereas usability and security receive less attention~\cite{ayeswarya2024review}; we therefore report false locks per hour and the time the guard needs to act. These streams test the decision logic over many hours and strangers, but they do not reproduce continuous head motion, exposure adaptation, autofocus, natural occlusion or tracking instability, and their owner never leaves the desk; the walk-away rule is therefore tested only by the real-world evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:pilot}. In the \emph{owner at the desk} scenario, \gOwners{} people with at least 20 images were enrolled with 10 images each and then shown their remaining photographs for 1--6~s each, \gMinutes{} minutes per owner; since these photographs vary in age, lighting and pose, the task is harder than facing a webcam at a fixed desk. The owner was recognized in \gMatchFrac\% of frames and labeled unknown in \gUnknownFrac\% (Table~\ref{tab:guard}), and the persistence and quality rules avoided any false intruder lock in \gHours{}~h, which bounds the false-lock rate by \gLockBound{} per hour (rule of three). No walk-away lock occurred even without typing: an authorized face was present in every frame (longest period without one: \gLongestGap~s, against $T_a=30$~s), so the frames labeled unknown are frames in which the largest face in view was another person in the photograph. The guard did issue \gShldAlerts{} shoulder-surfer alerts (\gShldAlertsHour{} per hour). Re-running the production engine on the streamed photographs traced them to the \gdSecond{} of \gdPhotos{} images (\gdSecondPct\%) in which a second, unknown person at least 40~pixels wide is visible, a common sight in the news photographs from which LFW was collected~\cite{huang2007lfw}. These alerts are thus correct under the rule, but they reveal that it flags anyone in view; cues such as gaze direction or distance could make it more selective.

\subsection{Real-World Evaluation}\label{sec:pilot}
Table~\ref{tab:pilot} lists all sessions of both rounds. Round~1 (\rwPilotTrials{} sessions, earlier protocol) immediately exposed faults that the simulation had missed: only \rwPilotOwnerPass{} of the owner's \rwPilotOwner{} genuine face-unlock sessions passed. In \rwPilotDiff{} sessions the identity check rejected the owner, who had turned their head by $31^\circ$--$49^\circ$, where the embedding differs from the frontal reference, pose being one of the main difficulties of face recognition~\cite{gupta2024ensemble}; in \rwPilotBlink{} sessions natural blinks, the cue of eyeblink-based liveness detection with a generic webcam~\cite{pan2007eyeblink}, were missed because, at the 10 frames per second that the webcam delivered at $1280\times720$, a blink was sampled at peak scores of only 0.23--0.39; and in \rwPilotNoResp{} sessions the owner did not start the requested turn within 3.5~s, much later than simple visual reaction times~\cite{tatsuno2016reaction}, because the prompt has to be read first. We corrected the first two faults (identity check within $20^\circ$ of the baseline pose, blink thresholds 0.30 and 0.20, camera at $640\times480$), kept the response window, re-ran the simulations of Section~\ref{sec:liveres} with the corrected protocol, and ran round~2 with the same tasks.

The physical ablation of the gate is therefore inconclusive: the turn-only protocol rejected all \rwAttTurnsGate{} attacks with the parallax gate and all \rwAttTurnsNoGate{} without it, because no phone attack reached the turn threshold at which the gate acts. In these attacks the yaw threshold, the identity check and the expression prompts, not the parallax gate, were decisive, and the strongly tilted print of Section~\ref{sec:pose} remains to be reproduced physically. All attacks were also made by one operator with one phone, one photograph and one room, so they are repeated measurements of a single attack instrument rather than independent attack opportunities: the per-attempt bound above is optimistic, and zero observed successes do not show that the attack probability is zero. The guard locked the PC \rwWalkBeep~s after the walk-away cue, 30~s after the owner's face was last seen as configured, because the owner had left the camera's view shortly before the cue; with a single physical walk-away event, the reliability of this rule across users, distances, departure directions and early returns remains to be measured. Wearable deauthentication such as ZEBRA~\cite{mare2014zebra} or FADEWICH~\cite{conti2017fadewich} reacts within seconds of a departure but needs extra hardware, whereas the camera-only rule trades latency for none. In the volunteer scenarios it acted in every trial. A stranger who sat down was locked out a median of \rwIntrBeep~s after the owner left (\rwIntrLat~s after the stranger was first detected): with the third volunteer the lock took 4.8--8.6~s, close to the simulation (Table~\ref{tab:guard}), whereas with the first two it took 21--28~s, because their live scores repeatedly fell into the uncertain band between 0.30 and 0.45, which interrupts the 2.5~s confirmation of an unknown face. In the timed trial the alert came \rwShldBeep~s after the cue, because the volunteer standing behind the owner was labeled unknown in only 13 of 87 frames. These results confirm the behavior of \sys{} under real conditions and expose its failure modes, and the Wilson intervals in Table~\ref{tab:pilot} quantify their uncertainty.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{Security position.} \sys{} only adds protection. A false reject locks a present owner, who then enters the PIN, and a false accept prevents a lock, leaving the OS as it would have been without \sys{}; access to the OS is never granted. Severe degradations lowered the false-accept rate (Section~\ref{sec:robust}), the safety valves prevent lock-out loops, and encrypted templates without stored video limit the privacy exposure, the concern that also drives template protection in cloud-based biometric systems~\cite{prabhu2022stego}. Because other applications share the camera, the guard can be paused during video calls, and it releases the camera while the PC is locked unless the lock-screen watch is enabled.

\emph{Levels of evidence.} Analytically, a planar presentation produces zero homography parallax for an ideal pinhole camera and exact landmarks (Proposition~1). In simulation, with the production code, held-out face meshes and 1-pixel landmark noise, the parallax gate removes the tilted-print weakness of the yaw ratio, and order enforcement with response timing makes universal replay several times less successful than in conventional protocols; these results hold over independent seeds and a range of thresholds, but weaken as landmark noise grows. Physically, the protocol rejected every phone-screen attack, but the contribution of the gate itself was not demonstrated: the learned landmark detector reported parallax for tilted photographs that grows with their apparent yaw, and no attack reached the turn threshold at which the gate acts. The liveness check of \sys{} should therefore be read as a challenge-response protocol that resisted non-interactive print and replay presentations in simulation and phone-screen presentations in our physical tests, not as general protection against presentation attacks.

\emph{Limits of the liveness check.} RGB challenge-response cannot stop replays that follow the prompts in real time, injected video, bent prints in turn-only mode or 3-D masks, so sensitive resources should add infrared sensing, which is insensitive to illumination and reveals disguises~\cite{athira2018thermal}, depth sensing, a passive learned detector, ideally one trained for generalization to unseen domains~\cite{liu2018auxiliary,wang2022ssan}, or a hardware-backed camera path. We did not compare the protocol with learned RGB presentation-attack detectors, because the real-world sessions stored no images; such a comparison under identical physical attacks, reporting APCER, BPCER, their average (ACER)~\cite{iso30107-3}, latency and computational cost, is needed to judge the geometric cue against current detectors. The physical attacks also used a single phone, photograph, operator and webcam: flat and curved prints of different materials, several screens and screen sizes, replayed video, and cameras other than the one on which the thresholds were frozen are needed to find which physical properties break the planarity assumption. Lens distortion is not among them for typical laptop webcams (Section~\ref{sec:pose}); landmarks that track image motion instead of predicting face geometry are the most promising way to restore the separation that the ideal model predicts.

\emph{Validity.} The liveness and guard rate estimates are from simulation with rendered landmarks, a behavioral model and composited photographs. The held-out calibration, the repeated runs and the sensitivity analyses show that the conclusions do not hinge on the fitted constants, the thresholds or a particular behavioral setting, although they do depend on the landmark-noise model, and the real-world evaluation confirmed the qualitative behavior, but it also showed what simulation misses: real users turn their heads further and react later than modeled, low webcam frame rates blunt blinks, first-time users confuse left and right, tilted photographs produce more parallax than the landmark-noise model predicts, and live impostor scores can hover in the uncertain band and delay intruder locks. The guard was evaluated on composited streams and on a few real events, including a single walk-away; a continuous real-camera study with several users, repeated departures and returns, bystanders, occlusions and changes of illumination, reporting false and missed locks per hour and per event, is needed before its reliability can be claimed. Recognition on laptop-webcam images was evaluated with only 15 identities, and the demographic analysis relies on estimated labels on an unbalanced dataset, a known source of bias in such studies~\cite{drozdowski2020bias}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented \sys{}, a CPU-only system that adds face recognition, a liveness check and continuous presence authentication to ordinary webcams while leaving OS sign-in untouched, and studied a minimal homography-parallax test for planar presentations within it. Analytically, we showed that perspective-induced roll leakage lets tilted prints pass five-landmark head-turn checks and that the residual of the nose under the homography of the other four landmarks vanishes for any planar presentation under an ideal pinhole camera with exact landmarks. In Monte-Carlo simulations of the production code on held-out face meshes, the test combined with ordered, timed challenges stopped every simulated print attack and reduced the success of non-interactive replays well below that of conventional protocols, consistently over independent seeds and thresholds, whereas replays that follow the prompts in real time passed in most sessions. Physically, a laptop-webcam evaluation with \rwAllSessions{} recorded sessions rejected all \rwAllAttN{} phone-screen attacks and revealed two faults that simulation had missed, which we corrected; it also showed that a learned landmark detector reports parallax for tilted photographs that grows with their apparent yaw, so in these attacks the protection came from the other checks rather than from the parallax gate. The recognition pipeline, chosen for its license and CPU cost rather than its accuracy, reaches \lfwAccFull\% on LFW and an EER of \mpEer\% on laptop-webcam images recorded over weeks, with a higher false-match rate for women than for men across three networks. Future work includes landmarks that measure image motion, physical print, display, replay and mask attacks with several cameras and users, a comparison with learned presentation-attack detectors, a continuous real-camera study of the guard, and a demographically balanced evaluation.
